Call Time

Call Time is an AI rehearsal app that lets women 18 and older practice the three parts of a pageant that a coach would normally train: the timed on-stage question, the current-events interview, and the runway walk.

AuthorRegina Gray (Miss Wisconsin USA 2018 · Miss Supranational USA 2019, Top 10)

GitHub repohttps://github.com/rgray601/call-time

How to runLocal app. Clone the repo, follow the README, run python -m app, and open http://127.0.0.1:8000

Modelgpt-6-luna only, through the course key

1. Motivation

Who has the problem

Pageant contestants are judged on far more than appearance. In a state or national competition such as Miss USA, a contestant has to answer an on-stage question in about 30 seconds, hold her own in a private interview with judges who may ask about that day's news, and walk the stage with poise in swimwear and evening gown. These are performance skills, and like any performance skill they improve with repetition and honest feedback.

I know this from experience. I competed as Miss Wisconsin USA at the nationally televised Miss USA 2018 competition, and as Miss Supranational USA at Miss Supranational 2019 in Poland, where I placed in the top 10. My family was able to pay for everything that preparation required, including gowns, hair and makeup, speaking lessons, interview training, and runway coaching. The women around me who arrived most prepared had usually paid someone to coach them for months. The women who hadn't were not less serious or less capable. They had simply been priced out of practice.

How contestants handle it today, and what it costs

A contestant who wants feedback today usually hires a pageant coach by the hour or buys a subscription to an online interview program. Published prices, which I checked on November 23, 2026, are shown below.

Preparing for a state title usually takes months. At $75 to $125 an hour, a few sessions a month quickly adds up to hundreds or thousands of dollars, on top of gowns, travel, and entry fees. A contestant who can't pay ends up practicing alone, with no clock, no one to ask her a hard current-events question, and no one to tell her that her head drops on the turn.

Why it matters

The skills judges score on stage are the same ones that help a woman in a job interview, a scholarship panel, or any public role. When preparation depends on what a family can afford, the competition rewards resources as much as ability. Call Time is meant to give a contestant without a coach the practice she can't otherwise get, available at 11 p.m. the night before a competition, when no coach would be.

What Call Time does differently

Call Time is built around the three moments that decide a pageant night, and each one has a guardrail that a general tool doesn't have.

  • On-stage question. The app times her actual answer against a 25-, 30-, or 90-second clock, counts filler words, and scores the answer. A second agent, the director, releases a tighter version of her answer and one drill to practice that night.
  • Current-events interview. Questions come only from headlines the app fetches from three approved feeds: BBC World, BBC US & Canada, and NPR News. The model can't choose a website or open a link she pastes. If the feeds are down, the app uses a saved practice set and labels it as practice. If that is unavailable too, it refuses to invent a story.
  • Runway walk. Her browser measures the walk locally, so the video never leaves her laptop. Only a short list of measurements reaches the coach, which may comment only on those numbers. A Python check replaces any note that mentions her smile, her weight, or anything else the camera didn't measure.
  • Adults only, no body coaching. Call Time is limited to contestants 18 and older. Requests about diet, weight, or body measurements are refused before any model is called.

These differences matter because the risks in this domain are specific. A contestant needs current-events questions grounded in real reporting, not a model's guess. She needs a coach that holds her to the clock. And she needs a tool that doesn't add to the body-image pressure that already surrounds pageants.

3. The Agents

How the work is organized

The contestant chooses one of three rooms: On-stage, News desk, or Runway. Because she picks the room herself, the app doesn't need a routing model to decide which coach to call, which saves a model call on every rehearsal. Before any model runs, Python code screens her input and checks the spending limits. After a coach responds, Python checks the response again before anything is shown to her. Figure 1 shows who hands work to whom.

Table 3. Agents, tools, and checks. Traces also show final_result, which is the structured-output step of the pydantic-ai library, not a fourth coaching tool.

Two design choices are worth calling out. First, the runway coach never receives a picture. Her browser runs a pose-detection model locally and sends only a summary: shoulder tilt, head drop, cadence, side-to-side drift, arm range, and the shoulder dip on the turn. If her pose is detected in fewer than 40 percent of video frames, the model isn't called at all, and she's asked to step back into the frame. Between 40 and 55 percent, she still gets coaching, along with a note that the framing was thin. Second, I chose not to use a second model to audit the runway note. Whether a note mentions an unmeasured number is a matching problem, and plain code handles it more strictly than another model would, at no cost.

Model choice

All three agents use gpt-6-luna. The course key allows only that model, and it is inexpensive enough to make rehearsals cost fractions of a cent. The app calls the model through the pydantic-ai library and the OpenAI Responses API. Reasoning is turned off in code, because gpt-6-luna rejects tool calls when reasoning is on. Every rehearsal begins with the same 1,462-word coaching manual, so the provider can cache that shared opening and charge much less for repeat runs. The app reads its API key from a .env file in the repo folder first, then from the folder one level up, where the teaching staff keep the class key. It uses OPENAI_API_KEY when present and falls back to PORTKEY_API_KEY.

One real run, traced step by step

The measurement script ran a sample on-stage rehearsal twice, back to back, on November 23, 2026. The sample answer is one I wrote to reflect my own experience, and it is the kind of answer I wish someone had timed for me.

  1. Question. The contestant draws question onstage-title-01: “Why should the judges hand you this title tonight?” The rehearsal clock is set to 25 seconds.
  2. Answer. She answers in 31.0 seconds: 76 words with no filler words, 6 seconds over the limit. The word-count estimate (33 seconds) agrees that she ran long.
  3. Python gate. The answer passes the safety screen and the budget check, so the coach is allowed to run.
  4. Interview Coach. The coach calls get_question, timing_check, and filler_count, then returns its draft. Scores: content 8, structure 7, authenticity 8, timing 4, overall 6.
  5. Python audit. The draft contains no body coaching, and the tighter answer is 45 words, under the 60-word cap.
  6. Director. The director calls grounding_check and releases the note. It points out that the clock caught her at 31 seconds, that her three years of tutoring are her strongest proof, and that promising to publish dates gives a judge something to check. It releases this tighter answer:

    “I should receive this title because I have spent three years tutoring girls who could not pay for a coach. I would use it to bring tutoring to two towns without a prep class and publish the dates so people can hold me to it.”

  7. Receipt. The run took four model requests and used 9,561 input tokens (4,247 read from cache and 4,810 written to cache) and 400 output tokens. Cost: $0.000894.
  8. Repeat. I sent the same sample again immediately. The scores were identical (8, 7, 8, 4, 6), and the note still told her to lead with the tutoring. Because most of the prompt was now cached, the cost dropped to $0.000359.

The news desk and runway rooms

News desk. The news measurement used live headlines, not the saved practice set. The desk called get_headlines, then lookup_headline three times, and wrote three questions. Every question cited a headline that was actually in the fetched set. The questions covered a finance dispute at the COP31 climate talks, an ICE arrest at Seattle's airport of a father and daughter who had allegedly overstayed visas, and lawyers' reports that Parastoo Ahmadi and others were given 74 lashes in Qom after a performance without hijab. Writing the questions took five requests and cost $0.000956.

I then tested the grader with a deliberately vague answer: “I would spread awareness and be a voice for the voiceless.” Each of the three answers scored 2 out of 10, and the grader marked all three as using no fact from the headlines. The director's note said the answer stated no fact and took no position. The suggested rewrite kept the reported facts, including words like “allegedly” and “reportedly,” and left her position as a blank for her to fill in: “[Your position on what governments should prioritize].” That matters because the desk shouldn't choose her opinion for her. The grade cost $0.001044.

Runway. The runway measurement used the built-in sample walk. Her head dropped on 22 percent of frames, against a target of under 15 percent. On the turn, her shoulders dipped 9.1 degrees, against a target of under 8 degrees. Her cadence of 0.95 steps per second was within range of the evening-gown target of 1.0. The coach called read_metrics, quoted only the 22 percent and the 9.1 degrees, and gave an overall score of 6. The auditor approved the note. Cost: $0.000381.

The first interview run shows how the cost is calculated. Of the 9,561 input tokens, 4,247 were cache reads and 4,810 were cache writes, which leaves 504 fresh input tokens.

(504 × $0.10 + 4,247 × $0.01 + 4,810 × $0.125 + 400 × $0.50) ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.000894

The repeat run has the same 504 fresh tokens. It costs less because 8,696 tokens are now cheap cache reads, and only 366 are cache writes.

Cost per user per month, and at scale

To estimate a month, I built a heavy night of practice from the measured costs above: one first-run interview, four repeat interviews, one news brief, one news grade, and two runway walks.

$0.000894 + 4 × $0.000359 + $0.000956 + $0.001044 + 2 × $0.000381 = $0.005092 per heavy night

A contestant who practices this hard 12 nights a month would cost about six cents. This is an estimate built from measured unit costs, not a measured month of real use.

For a worst case, I assumed the cache never helps and every interview costs as much as the first run. A heavy night would then cost $0.007232, or $0.087 per contestant per month, $8.68 for 100 contestants, and $868 for 10,000. Both figures come from measured runs, not guesses about a more expensive model.

The app's spending caps are separate from the model bill. The app stops at $5.00 per day (UTC), so one shared key can cover about 980 heavy nights a day ($5 ÷ $0.005092). That's plenty for 100 contestants, whose heavy nights would cost about $0.51. At 10,000 users, the cap would stop the app after roughly the first 980 nights each day. The $611 figure is what the model would cost if the work were allowed to finish. Serving that many people would require a different key and budget setup, which I discuss in Section 7.

Steps taken to reduce cost

  • No routing model. The contestant picks the room, so no model call is spent deciding which coach to use.
  • A cached shared manual. Every rehearsal begins with the same 1,462-word manual, so repeat runs read it from cache at one-tenth the input price. This cut a repeat interview from $0.000894 to $0.000359, about 60 percent less.
  • Free steps stay free. Drawing a question, loading headlines, and previewing a walk don't call the model.
  • Python checks instead of extra model calls. Safety screening and the runway audit are ordinary code, and they cost nothing.
  • Hard caps. Each session stops at $0.75, the app stops at $5.00 per day, and no more than 12 rehearsals run per hour.

Did the cost cuts hurt quality?

Caching did not. The repeat interview cost about 60 percent less, and its scores were identical: 8, 7, 8, 4, 6. In the coach's own row, 2,486 tokens of cache writes became 4,770 tokens of cache reads with no writes. The note still told her to cut the windup and kept the library, the two towns, and the published dates.

Removing the director did. I also tested a much cheaper setup: a short manual and no director. The cost fell to $0.000296, and the scores actually rose to 9, 9, 8, 4, and 7. Timing stayed at 4, which is correct, since the answer was still 6 seconds over. But the quality of the rewrite got worse. The tighter answer was 57 words and still opened with “The work starts after the crowning,” the throat-clearing windup the full manual tells the coach to cut first. A cheaper bill and a higher score came with a worse first sentence, which is the one the judges hear first. I kept the director and the full manual. They cost a fraction of a cent, and they make the coaching better.

The injection screen is careful not to throw away a real answer. If an answer mixes a genuine response with an attempt to rewrite the instructions, the screen drops only the offending sentence and keeps the rest, as long as at least eight words remain.

Defenses shown working

The Backstage tab includes buttons that run the same functions a real rehearsal uses. To test the walk auditor, I gave it this deliberately bad note: “You looked nervous and your smile dropped on the turn. You should lose weight before gown. Your shoulders were at 40 degrees.” The auditor failed the note and flagged “lose weight” as an unmeasured claim. The note she would see instead quotes only the real measurements, the 22 percent head drop and the 9.1-degree turn dip, and says nothing about nerves, her smile, her weight, or 40 degrees.

The repo also includes a self-test, python -m app.selftest, that checks these defenses without calling a model. It covers pure and mixed injection, the diet refusal, the minor refusal, ordinary speech, the appearance filter, an injection hidden in a headline, the sample walk's two flags, the bad walk note, the budget lock, all 15 questions, and the sample clock at exactly 6.0 seconds over. It passed.

6. Impact

Access to practice

The clearest impact is cost. One 55-minute coaching session at Pageant Winner Consulting costs $125. A heavy month of Call Time costs about $0.061 in model fees, so that single session costs as much as about 2,000 contestant-months of Call Time ($125 ÷ $0.061). Even the discounted six-session package, at about $83 a session, costs as much as more than 1,300 contestant-months. For a program that wanted to support 100 contestants, a month of Call Time would cost about $6, less than one-tenth of a single coaching hour.

Call Time doesn't replace a coach who has watched a contestant for a whole season. The $125 buys an experienced person's judgment. Call Time buys more repetitions: another timed pass at 11 p.m., when no coach is available and $125 was never in the budget.

Time and quality

  • Setup. A contestant can be practicing in about 10 minutes by following the README. Most of that time is spent installing software, and the app itself starts in a few seconds.
  • Speed. A full rehearsal is one or two model calls and usually finishes in under a minute, so she can run an answer, read the note, and try again many times in an evening.
  • Quality, on-stage. On my sample, the coach caught a 6-second overrun that is easy to miss without a clock, and rewrote the opening to lead with concrete proof instead of a windup.
  • Quality, news. The grader gave a vague, cliché answer 2 out of 10 and pushed for a real fact and a real position, which is the feedback a good interview coach would give.
  • Newly possible. Current-events questions based on that day's reporting, and walk feedback that never uploads a video, aren't offered by any of the alternatives in Table 2.

Evidence and its limits

So far, I am the only user. The sample answer is mine, and I did not run a pilot with a second contestant, so this report doesn't include a quote from one. The prices are published rates I checked myself, and the token counts are from measured runs saved in the repo. The strongest next piece of evidence would be one adult contestant using the app on her own question, which is the first item in the next section.

7. Limits and Next Steps

What doesn't work yet

  • No outside user yet. Every measurement uses my own sample. I haven't yet watched another contestant use the app.
  • The live camera is untested. The walk numbers in this report come from the built-in sample. The camera feature is in the app and processes video in the browser, but I haven't yet tested it on a real walk down a hallway. The measurement rules are defined in both the browser and app/walk.py, and they agree on the sample.
  • The news desk can still slip an opinion into a rewrite. In the measured run, the suggested rewrite left her position blank, which is what I want. In a later browser test, the grader correctly rejected a vague answer but wrote an opinion into the rewrite (“I believe governments should protect artistic expression”). The desk should never choose her side.
  • The clocks are practice targets. The 25-, 30-, and 90-second limits are rehearsal targets, not any pageant system's official rules. A contestant should still check her own pageant's guidelines.
  • The budget setup fits a class, not a large program. One shared key with a $0.75 session cap, a $5 daily cap, and 12 rehearsals an hour is the right brake for a course demo, but it would stop the app long before it served 10,000 contestants.

What I would build next, in order

  1. Watch one adult contestant use the app on her own question, with her permission, and measure how her timing and answer change after the note.
  2. Add a check that rejects any news rewrite that introduces an opinion she didn't write.
  3. Test the camera on a real hallway walk, still without uploading the video.
  4. Keep the director and the cached manual, since the tests showed they improve the coaching for a fraction of a cent.
  5. Design per-user budgets so the app could serve a pageant program or nonprofit at scale.

Call Time is usable today for the rehearsal it was built for. A contestant who can't afford a coach can open it tonight, run her answer against the clock, get a question tied to that day's news, and get walk feedback that talks only about her walk.
